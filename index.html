Beyond CompressionThe Physics of Action Tokenization

As frontier LLM agents take over high-level planning, the action layer beneath them decides whether semantic plans actually succeed in physical contact and across different robot bodies.

Frontier LLM agents such as Opus-class reasoning models have rapidly become reliable high-level planners for robotics. Because task decomposition and symbolic reasoning now work well at the top of the stack, we think the decisive bottleneck in embodied AI has shifted down to the action layer. In practice, this layer serves as the contract that turns a planner's semantic intent into smooth and forceful control across different physical robots.

Most research on action tokenizers still evaluates them by how tightly they reconstruct demonstrations offline.1,4,6 Holding a single Qwen3-VL-2B backbone fixed across nine controlled experiments, we observe that low offline error tells us surprisingly little about closed-loop control. Below, we walk through five concrete mechanisms—each paired with an interactive 3D study—explaining why action representations succeed or fail in the real world and what we think builders should take away when designing an action stack beneath an LLM agent.

Chapter I · Token Order & Latency

Token order decides whether a policy can stop decoding early

When a frontier LLM agent updates a plan in real time, the action decoder beneath it often needs to cut latency without retraining.7,8,9,10 We observe that whether a policy survives early truncation depends almost entirely on how the tokenizer orders information across positions. In a global spectral codec like FAST,1 early tokens carry global frequency components, so dropping later tokens or perturbing one early token distorts the entire trajectory. By contrast, ordering tokens causally along time in Cover4 lets the decoder stop halfway through a chunk—running 1.85× faster while preserving closed-loop execution—because the prefix already defines the exact steps the robot executes first. Similarly, ordering tokens from coarse to fine in OAT11 holds 80% success at half the tokens because early slots already capture the main motion shape.

Our takeaway is straightforward: we think action vocabularies should adopt causal or coarse-to-fine ordering so upper-level agents can trade decoding compute for reaction speed on demand.

Chapter II · Coverage & Failure Diagnosis

Validation loss misses trajectory diversity and physical contact

Training six tokenizers on the same Qwen3-VL-2B backbone reveals a striking disconnect between training curves and real execution. Following recent findings on policy post-training dynamics,2 we observe that validation cross-entropy begins rising midway through training even as closed-loop performance keeps improving. This happens because cross-entropy penalizes multimodal uncertainty harshly, whereas maintaining an exponential moving average of model weights preserves diverse valid trajectories—lifting stochastic pass@4 coverage to 93% compared with 84% for raw weights. Tracing 200 live rollouts explains why capable policies still miss tasks: 72% of failures happen directly inside physical contact, where the gripper either presses against an object without moving or slips repeatedly during grasp.

We think this yields a clear diagnostic takeaway: selecting checkpoints by validation loss hides both multimodal diversity and physical contact failure, so tracking stochastic pass@k alongside contact-level rollout traces gives a far truer signal of policy readiness.

Chapter III · Cross-Embodiment Semantics

Low compression overhead on a new robot hides separate joint dialects

For one frontier LLM agent to direct different robots smoothly, the action layer needs shared physical meaning rather than just shared token IDs. Fitting a joint-space covering vocabulary across thirteen arms compresses unseen robots with minimal extra bitrate, which makes joint tokenization appear universal at first glance. Inspecting 900 paired trajectories across five arms explains why this transfer breaks down in practice: because every arm has its own link lengths and joint axes, the exact same 3D reach maps into completely different joint-angle tokens, locking each robot into its own cluster with 66% robot-ID purity. Moving those exact motions into canonical task space dissolves the five dialects down to the 22% random-mixing floor and lifts cross-arm 3D motion alignment nearly threefold.

We think this geometric split explains why recent cross-embodiment systems such as RoboMorpher12 and CIALLO14 supervise 3D link geometry directly or fit fast per-robot calibrators.13,15,16,17,18,19,20 The practical takeaway is that bitrate alone never certifies cross-robot transfer; shared semantics require grounding action tokens in a canonical 3D frame.

Chapter IV · Impedance Contact Physics

Replaying achieved poses erases contact force in impedance control

The physical contact stalls seen in Chapter II trace back to a fundamental property of compliant robot controllers.21,22 Under impedance control, a robot generates pushing or pulling force purely from the virtual spring deflection between its commanded target and its measured pose (p_cmd − p_meas). When we tokenize and replay achieved poses from a demonstration, the gripper follows the recorded path within a remarkable 0.29 mm error—yet because the command now sits right on top of the measured pose, the virtual spring stays completely flat and the drawer remains locked shut. Separating kinematic motion from a sparse deadband-gated Newton force channel (C1F+D3F) resolves this cleanly. Firing on just 31% of frames while scaling automatically by each robot's stiffness K_p, this sparse channel reaches 82% cross-arm drawer rescue and outperforms a dense force stream, because deadband gating keeps free-space motion crisp while delivering exact force during contact.

Our takeaway for cross-embodiment robotics is direct: pose-only action tokens discard the physics of forceful interaction, whereas a sparse Newton channel lets an upper-level planner command contact tasks reliably across robots with completely different stiffness gains.

Chapter V · Local Reflexes Beneath LLM Agents

Separating free-space drift from contact force lets the action layer self-correct

The same spring-deflection physics from Chapter IV also explains how an action layer should adapt online when unexpected execution drift appears. In free space, a steady gap between commanded motion and achieved motion reveals true kinematic drift. Once the gripper touches an object, however, that same gap reflects intentional contact force. Updating a drift estimator blindly mixes the two signals and creates drag during grasping. By gating residual updates strictly to free space—freezing estimation across 75% of contact chunks—a frozen policy under unknown lateral drift climbs from 32% open-loop success to 57% at zero extra reasoning tokens.

We think this clarifies the natural division of labor in the era of frontier LLM agents. Models such as Opus-class planners5,24 handle high-level reasoning and episodic reflection cleanly at low frequency, while the action stack beneath them—combining causal discrete bridges,4,11 smooth continuous motion experts like MR03 and SANA-Robot,23 and high-frequency force-and-drift reflexes—absorbs timing, geometry, contact force, and minor bias locally. The takeaway for system designers is clear: a physically grounded action layer shields the top-level LLM agent from noisy low-level physics and escalates upward only when genuine semantic replanning is needed.

Epilogue · Open Frontiers

What the action layer still needs to serve frontier LLM agents

Looking ahead, we think three open physical problems will shape how well action layers support frontier reasoning agents. Extending canonical 3D grounding and sparse force channels to multi-fingered dexterous hands requires factored synergies so token lengths stay compact as degrees of freedom grow. Adding morphology-aware reachability critics can inform an upper-level planner before it requests poses that lie outside a specific robot's kinematic workspace. Finally, connecting the persistent episodic memory of LLM agents5,24 with continual low-level motor consolidation will allow robots to turn real-world physical experience into lasting skill.
