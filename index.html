Action Tokenization (of What?)

An empirical handbook on what discrete action streams need to encode so Vision-Language-Action models execute reliably across embodiments and physical contact.

Vision-Language-Action models have rapidly adopted discrete action tokens as the core interface linking pretrained multimodal backbones to physical robot control.1,4,6 Driven by non-neural streaming codecs such as ActionCodec24 alongside morphology-aware encoders like RoboMorpher12 and CIALLO,14 the community can now compress trajectories across dozens of robots with minimal reconstruction error.

Within this fast-moving landscape, we observe an important gap between offline compression metrics and real closed-loop manipulation. Evaluating tokenizers primarily by open-loop mean-squared error leaves four decisive physical factors largely hidden: mid-stream decoding structure, embodiment-invariant 3D grounding, impedance contact force, and online drift separation. Closing this gap matters deeply for generalist robotics, because scaling VLM backbones amplifies whatever physical information the action vocabulary preserves.

We think the central design question has shifted from compressing trajectories tighter toward deciding which physical signals an action token stream should represent. Holding a single Qwen3-VL-2B backbone constant across nine controlled studies, we isolate each layer in sequence—tracing how causal decoding, canonical task space, sparse Newton force pulses, and contact-gated feedback converge into one coherent physical law.

Section 01 · Decoding Geometry

Causal and nested token orders enable anytime execution.

Starting from the lowest layer of representation, we first examine how a token stream behaves when inference compute is cut mid-flight or when a single token shifts.7,8,9,10 Lightweight spectral and geometric codecs already surpass heavy neural autoencoders across the rate–distortion curve.1,4,6 Consequently, closed-loop responsiveness depends directly on how information distributes across token positions during autoregressive generation.

We observe two complementary structures that support graceful budget reduction while keeping policy weights frozen. Ordering tokens causally along time allows Cover (EEF) to stop at step 16—halving generation from 50.3 to 25.2 tokens per chunk for a 1.85× speedup (143.2 → 77.2 ms) while retaining 76.0% LIBERO-Spatial success alongside 84.0% at full length. Ordering tokens progressively from coarse to fine allows OAT (Joint)11 to step down from 16 to 8 tokens (19.0 ms) while holding 80.0% success, confirming the value of matching runtime budgets to nested codec boundaries.

Section 02 · Policy Dynamics & Failure Anatomy

EMA preserves trajectory coverage while physical contact drives failure.

Following our analysis of decoding structure, we next train six distinct tokenizers under the exact same Qwen3-VL-2B recipe to see how offline loss translates into live LIBERO manipulation. Across four suites, Cover (EEF) reaches 73.9% and OAT (Joint) reaches 60.5%, whereas Bin256 achieves only 17.0% despite near-lossless 1.20 mm reconstruction. Tracking training trajectories further echoes recent findings on post-training dynamics:2 as validation cross-entropy climbs past 12k steps, maintaining exponential moving average weights lifts stochastic pass@4 coverage to 93.3% compared with 84.0% for raw weights.

Tracing 200 live rollouts step by step reveals why capable policies still miss episodes. In practice, 72.2% of failed rollouts (57 / 79) concentrate inside two concrete physical regimes: contact stalls (40.5%), where the gripper pushes into an object while motion stays below 1.5 mm, alongside repeated grasp slips (31.6%). This diagnosis leads us directly toward the two physical foundations examined next: shared spatial grounding across arms and explicit force encoding during contact.

Section 03 · Cross-Embodiment Grounding

Shared token syntax separates robots; task space unites them.

Extending our inquiry from a single arm to multi-embodiment transfer, we test whether a shared joint-slot vocabulary yields shared motion semantics. Compressing held-out arms with a joint-slot cover adds only +0.2% to +3.5% bitrate overhead across thirteen arms, suggesting apparent transfer at the syntax level. Inspecting 900 paired trajectories across five arms reveals the true underlying geometry: joint-slot tokens organize motions into five robot-specific islands with 66.0% 10-NN robot-ID purity, pulling zero-shot cross-arm 3D probe accuracy down to R² = −1.30.

Grounding the exact same trajectories in canonical task space (C1) unifies these islands immediately. Robot-ID purity relaxes to the 22.4% random-mixing baseline (20% ideal across five arms), while cross-arm 3D motion alignment rises nearly threefold from +0.243 to +0.693. We think this geometric principle explains a clear convergence across late-2026 robotics: RoboMorpher12 supervises 3D link poses directly (losing 17.94 pp when omitted), CIALLO14 calibrates new robots in ten seconds via a 174K adapter, and embodiment-aligned systems such as BiCAT,13 AIRoA-0,17 and UniLatent15 anchor control in shared physical frames.16,18,19,20

Section 04 · Contact Physics

Impedance force lives in command–state deflection, solved by sparse pulses.

Returning to the physical contact stalls uncovered in Section 2 leads directly to a subtle property of compliant impedance control.21,22 Under spring–damper tracking, contact force arises from the virtual displacement p_cmd − p_meas between the commanded setpoint and the measured gripper pose. Replaying achieved kinematic poses tracks the drawer trajectory within a remarkable 0.29 mm median error while reducing spring deflection to zero—causing Franka drawer success to drop from 83% to 14% and stalling unseen UR5e, IIWA, and Kinova Gen3 arms completely.

We solve this cross-embodiment contact paradox by separating kinematic motion from a sparse, deadband-gated Newton force channel (C1F+D3F). Because target force normalizes automatically by each robot's controller stiffness K_p, a 5 mm quantized pulse gated by a 12 mm deadband activates on only 30.8% of frames yet lifts three-arm drawer rescue to 81.7%—surpassing a 100% continuous dense force stream (80.0%) and raw OSC replay across stiffness shifts while leaving free-space precision completely untouched.

Section 05 · Closed-Loop Adaptation

One physical law closes the loop: free space reveals drift; contact reveals force.

The exact same command–state relationship that governs cross-arm contact also resolves online closed-loop adaptation under unknown execution drift. Measuring tracking residuals indiscriminately mixes kinematic bias with intentional grasp force, allowing blind proprioceptive feedback to rescue long k=32 chunks (32% → 52%) while introducing drag during contact. Gating residual updates strictly to free space freezes 74.7% of contact chunks, lifting a frozen Qwen3-VL-2B policy to 56.7% under +0.07 drift and 50.0% under severe +0.10 drift at zero extra VLM tokens.

Zooming back out to the full system level, we think this physical separation explains why modern robotic stacks naturally stratify into four complementary timescales. Sparse episodic agents such as RoboThink5 and AMP124 adapt across attempts with minimal token overhead; causal or nested discrete codecs4,11 anchor semantic pretraining; continuous motion experts like MR03 and SANA-Robot23 execute smooth 30 Hz trajectories; and high-frequency force executors translate sparse Newton intent and free-space feedback into compliant physical interaction.

Section 06 · Outlook & References

Three open frontiers for cross-embodiment tokenization.

Looking ahead, we think three concrete challenges define the next wave of physical action representations. Extending task-space and force-intent tokenization to 20–24 DoF dexterous hands requires factoring finger synergies to avoid the +48% to +154% token blowup seen in naive joint covers; incorporating morphology-aware kinematic reach critics can prevent infeasible plans such as Kinova3's 130/151 IK wall; and coupling episodic agentic memory5,24 with continual low-level policy consolidation offers a path toward robots that improve permanently through real-world interaction.
